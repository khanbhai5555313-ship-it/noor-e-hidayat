Investment in Islam: IPOs, Shares and Shariah Guidelines

Understanding Halal and Haram Investment Principles in Simple English

Home Quran Hadith Duas Articles
Important: This article is an educational guide about Islamic principles of investing in shares and IPOs. It does not declare any particular company halal or haram. A company should be reviewed using its latest business activities and financial information, and a qualified Shariah scholar should be consulted for a specific ruling.

Investing in shares and participating in Initial Public Offerings (IPOs) has become common in many countries. Muslims may therefore ask: Is investing in shares allowed in Islam?

The answer cannot always be given by looking only at the name of a company or at its share price. A Muslim investor should understand what the company does, how it earns money, whether it is involved in prohibited activities, and whether its financial structure contains prohibited elements such as riba.

This guide explains these principles and uses a current IPO topic, Lumino Industries, only as an example of how a Muslim should investigate an investment opportunity.

1. What Is Investment?

Investment means putting money into an asset or business with the expectation of receiving a lawful return in the future.

In the case of shares, an investor purchases an ownership interest in a company. The investor may benefit if the value of the shares rises or through dividends when the company distributes profits.

However, profit by itself does not automatically make an investment halal. The way the profit is generated and the business in which the money is invested are important.

2. What Is a Share?

A share represents an ownership interest in a company. A shareholder is therefore not simply lending money to the company in the same way as a person who gives an interest-bearing loan.

The shareholder participates in the ownership of the company and bears investment risk. The value of the share can increase or decrease, and dividends are not guaranteed.

Important distinction: A genuine ownership share should not be confused with an interest-bearing loan. Islamic finance strongly distinguishes between lawful trade and riba-based lending.

3. What Is an IPO?

IPO stands for Initial Public Offering. It is the process through which a company offers shares to the public and seeks to become publicly listed.

An IPO can involve newly issued shares, existing shareholders selling shares, or both.

From an Islamic perspective, the important question is not simply:

"Is this an IPO?"

Instead, the investor should ask:

  • What does the company actually do?
  • How does the company earn its income?
  • Does the company conduct prohibited activities?
  • Does its financial structure contain significant interest-based financing?
  • Does the investment satisfy an accepted Shariah screening methodology?

4. What Does Islam Say About Trade and Riba?

Qur'an — Surah Al-Baqarah 2:275

وَأَحَلَّ اللَّهُ الْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ الرِّبَا

Meaning: Allah has permitted trade and forbidden interest (riba).

Qur'an, Surah Al-Baqarah 2:275

This verse gives an important foundation: lawful trade and riba are not the same thing. Therefore, Islamic investment should be structured around lawful ownership and trade rather than prohibited interest.

Allah also commands believers to give up what remains of riba in Surah Al-Baqarah 2:278–279.

Read Surah Al-Baqarah 2:275–280

5. First Check: What Is the Company's Main Business?

The first and most important screening question is:

What does the company actually do?

If the company's core business is based on an activity that Islamic law clearly prohibits, buying its shares raises a fundamental Shariah problem.

Examples commonly excluded in Shariah stock-screening methodologies include:

  • Conventional interest-based banking
  • Conventional insurance
  • Alcohol production or trading
  • Gambling and betting
  • Pork-related businesses
  • Adult entertainment
  • Other businesses whose core activity is clearly prohibited

The exact treatment of borderline industries can differ between scholars and Shariah standards, so unusual cases should be reviewed separately.

6. Second Check: Interest and Riba

Riba is one of the most important issues in Islamic finance. A company may have a permissible main business but still use interest-bearing financing or receive interest income.

Sahih Hadith — Sahih Muslim 1598

Jabir رضي الله عنه reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ condemned the one who consumes riba, the one who pays it, the one who records it and the two witnesses, saying that they are all equal.

Sahih Muslim 1598

This hadith shows the seriousness of riba. Therefore, a Muslim investor should not ignore interest merely because a company has a profitable or popular business.

Read Sahih Muslim 1598

7. What If a Company Has Debt?

This is an area where people often make an overly simple conclusion.

A company having debt does not by itself tell us everything about its Shariah status. We need to know:

  • What type of debt is it?
  • Is it interest-bearing?
  • How large is it compared with the company's market value?
  • Does the company also hold interest-bearing investments or deposits?
  • How much income comes from prohibited sources?
Important: The fact that a company has conventional debt does not mean that a simple "loan = automatically haram stock" conclusion is sufficient for every contemporary Shariah screening framework. The financial structure must be examined according to the methodology being used.

8. Investment Is Not the Same as Gambling

Another important distinction is between investing and gambling.

A genuine investment involves ownership in an asset or business and exposure to the business's economic performance.

Gambling, betting and games of chance are fundamentally different: money is placed at risk for an uncertain gain based primarily on chance rather than legitimate ownership and productive economic activity.

Therefore, a Muslim investor should avoid gambling and betting and should also be careful with highly speculative trading practices.

Long-term investment vs excessive speculation

Not every short-term trade is automatically gambling. The details of the transaction matter. Questions of leverage, derivatives, short selling, options, futures and other products require separate Shariah analysis.

This article therefore focuses mainly on ordinary ownership of permissible company shares rather than every type of financial instrument.

9. Shariah Stock Screening

Contemporary Islamic finance uses Shariah screening methodologies to evaluate companies whose main business is permissible but whose financial statements may contain some non-compliant elements.

One widely referenced methodology is associated with AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 21. Published summaries of the methodology commonly describe three major financial screens:

  1. Interest-bearing debt: generally below 30% of market capitalization.
  2. Interest-bearing deposits/securities: generally below 30% of market capitalization.
  3. Non-permissible income: generally below 5% of total income/revenue.

These percentages are screening criteria used by contemporary Shariah methodologies; they are not numbers stated directly in the Qur'an or in the hadith quoted above.

Do not confuse the two: Qur'an and authentic Sunnah establish the prohibition of riba and other prohibited activities. Contemporary Shariah standards provide screening methodologies for evaluating modern corporations whose financial structures may contain mixed elements.

Different Islamic indices and scholars may use different details, denominators and thresholds. Therefore, a screening result should always state which methodology was used.

The purpose of mentioning Lumino here is not to issue a fatwa saying that Lumino Industries is halal or haram.

Instead, it gives us a practical example of how a Muslim investor should investigate any IPO.

Questions a Muslim investor should ask

  1. What is the company's main business?
  2. Does the company earn money from prohibited activities?
  3. Does it use interest-bearing debt?
  4. How much interest-bearing debt does it have?
  5. Does it hold interest-bearing deposits or securities?
  6. How much non-permissible income does it receive?
  7. Does it pass the Shariah screening methodology being followed?

This same checklist can be applied to future IPOs and listed companies.

11. Muslim Investor Checklist

✓ 1. Check the business
Make sure the company's main activity is permissible.
✓ 2. Read the official documents
For an IPO, check the company's official prospectus and financial statements rather than relying only on social media or WhatsApp.
✓ 3. Check interest-bearing debt
Identify conventional loans and other interest-bearing obligations.
✓ 4. Check interest-bearing investments
Look for conventional deposits, bonds and similar instruments.
✓ 5. Check prohibited income
Determine whether income from prohibited activities is material.
✓ 6. Check the screening methodology
Clearly identify which Shariah standard or screening methodology is being applied.
✓ 7. Avoid gambling and prohibited speculation
Do not confuse legitimate ownership with betting or gambling.
✓ 8. Ask a qualified scholar when necessary
Especially when the company's business or financial structure is complicated or disputed.

12. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: "The company makes a lot of profit, so it must be halal."

Profitability does not determine whether a business is Shariah-compliant.

Mistake 2: "The company has a loan, so it is automatically haram."

The type and amount of financing and the screening methodology must be examined before reaching a conclusion.

Mistake 3: "A halal stock website says it is halal, so no research is needed."

Screening services can be useful, but their methodology and data date should be checked. Important decisions should be reviewed with a qualified Shariah scholar.

Mistake 4: "The IPO is trending, so I should invest quickly."

A trending search is not evidence that an investment is good or halal. Google Trends measures search interest; it does not provide financial or Shariah approval.

Mistake 5: "The price is going up, so I cannot lose."

Share prices can fall as well as rise. Investment involves risk. Muslims should avoid greed, deception and prohibited forms of speculation.

13. A Simple Example

Imagine there are two companies.

Company A

  • Main business is a lawful product.
  • No prohibited core activity.
  • Financial structure passes the applicable Shariah screening.
  • Investment represents genuine shares/ownership.

Such a company may be considered for Shariah-compliant investment, subject to the applicable scholarly methodology.

Company B

  • Main business is gambling.
  • Its main revenue comes from betting activities.

In this situation, the problem is with the company's core business itself, so simply looking at its profits or debt ratios would not make the investment permissible.

14. What About Dividends and Purification?

Some Shariah screening methodologies allow a company with a small amount of non-permissible income to remain within the screening universe, while requiring purification of the impermissible portion received by investors.

The exact calculation and method of purification can differ depending on the Shariah standard and scholarly guidance being followed.

If a stock passes a Shariah screen, do not assume that every rupee of every dividend automatically requires the same treatment. Follow the purification calculation provided by the applicable Shariah authority or qualified scholar.

15. Conclusion

Islam does not prohibit business or lawful investment. The Qur'an clearly distinguishes lawful trade from riba.

For shares and IPOs, a Muslim investor should look beyond the company's popularity, share price or Google search trend.

The main questions are:

  1. Is the company's core business permissible?
  2. Does the company substantially engage in prohibited activities?
  3. Does its financial structure contain interest-based financing?
  4. Does it pass the relevant Shariah financial screens?
  5. Is the transaction genuine ownership rather than a prohibited financial arrangement?
  6. Is the investor avoiding gambling, deception and prohibited speculation?

A trending IPO is not automatically a halal investment, and a profitable company is not automatically a Shariah-compliant company.

The safest approach is to investigate the company's official documents, apply a clearly identified Shariah screening methodology, and consult a qualified Shariah scholar when a final ruling is needed.
